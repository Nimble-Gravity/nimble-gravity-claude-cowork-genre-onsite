# Competitive Positioning One-Pager

Turn a full peer-comparison research report (many pages, one per company, dense with figures) into
a single page a leader can actually read before a meeting: where Gen Re leads, where it's behind,
and what's genuinely uncertain because the underlying data doesn't support a clean comparison.

## When to run

- A peer-comparison research report already exists (see `reference/report-location.md` for where it's
  expected to live) and someone wants the leadership-readable summary version.
- Run this **after** the research report, never instead of it — this skill summarizes and ranks, it
  does not do its own primary research. If no report exists yet, say so and offer to run the research
  first rather than inventing positioning from memory.

## Inputs

- The research report (read from the working folder — ask where it lives if not obvious, don't guess
  a path).
- Optional: a specific quarter or subset of metrics to focus on, if the full report covers more than
  the reader needs.

## How to draft

1. **Read the whole report first**, not just the comparative-analysis section — the caveats and data
   gaps buried in each company's section matter for what you can honestly claim.
2. **Rank Gen Re against the peer set on each metric that has usable data** — combined ratio,
   underwriting profit/margin, premium growth, ROE, book value change, catastrophe-loss impact,
   wherever the report has comparable figures for both Gen Re and the peer group.
3. **Split findings into two lists**: where Gen Re is at or above the peer average/median, and where
   it's below. Lead with the strongest, most defensible lead — not the most flattering-sounding one.
4. **Carry forward every structural caveat from the source report** — most importantly, anywhere
   Gen Re's own figure is blended into a larger Berkshire segment (see the Berkshire 10-Q pattern) or
   otherwise not independently disclosed. A metric you can't actually isolate for Gen Re does not go
   in the "leading" or "behind" list — it goes in a **"can't be compared cleanly"** section, explicitly.
5. **Cite every claim** to the specific company and quarter it came from. No unsourced number, ever.
6. **One page.** If the honest answer needs more than a page, that's a sign to cut detail, not
   shrink the font — pick the 4-6 metrics that matter most for the audience this is going to.
7. **Close with next-quarter framing**, not a verdict: what would most change the picture if it moved
   (e.g. "if underwriting discipline on X continues, the gap on Y likely narrows"), stated as
   observation, not promise.

## Good vs. bad output

**Good** — leads with the clearest, sourced win; states "can't be cleanly compared" explicitly for
anything blended into Berkshire's consolidated segment reporting; every number traceable to a
company + quarter; reads like something a chief actuary could hand to leadership as-is.

**Bad** — presents a blended Berkshire segment figure as if it were Gen Re's own number; picks the
most flattering framing instead of the most defensible one; omits the comparability caveats the
source report already flagged; runs past one page.

## Guardrails

- Never treat this skill's own output as the source of truth — it is a summary of the research
  report, one level removed from the primary data. If a number in the report looks wrong, flag it
  rather than "smoothing" it into the one-pager.
- Never fabricate a peer figure to complete a ranking. Missing data → say it's missing.
- This produces a draft for internal review, not external content. See the separate
  `linkedin-post-drafting` skill for anything meant to leave the building — different guardrails
  apply there.
