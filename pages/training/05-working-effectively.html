Walk a Workflow

Today you take one real piece of recurring work — a quarterly competitive snapshot pulled from three source documents — and do it end to end with Cowork, by hand, remembering every step yourself. Nothing clever, nothing automated. The point is to feel exactly which parts you had to remember, because that is what Lesson 2 turns into something you never have to remember again.

Skills & Everyday Workflows · ~45 min
01 — Today's workflow

The competitive snapshot, done by hand

Every quarter, a few sources land — a peer's investor update, an internal filing excerpt, a request from leadership — and somebody wants one page saying how we compare. We're using this quarter's real, public numbers, so what you check the draft against is exactly as real as what you'd check on the job.

The sources

Three documents in day-2/ inside your workshop folder: munich-re-q2-update.docx, berkshire-insurance-segment.docx, and leadership-request-note.docx. Open them in Word, the same way you started the demo yesterday. Short enough to read in five minutes, which is the point — you can hold all three in your head while you check the draft.

Don't see them in day-2/? They were added after the workshop folder went out — download them from the Day 2 hub instead.

The deliverable

One page: how Gen Re's reinsurance underwriting compares against a major peer this quarter, sourced claim by claim. The kind of snapshot you'd hand to leadership before a check-in and be asked about five minutes later.

Why this one

It recurs every quarter, it has a fixed shape, and it's built entirely from documents that already exist — real ones, this time. That combination is what makes a workflow worth handing over. If you do something once a year and it's different every time, it isn't this.

02 — Steering while it works

Four habits for work that actually goes out

Three of these you met yesterday, and they matter more today, because today you're producing something a colleague would actually read rather than watching a demo go wrong on purpose. The fourth is new, and it is the reason the approval prompts are there at all.

01
Stay in "ask before acting"
Cowork pauses before each file it opens or writes and waits for you. Leave it there today. The pause is not friction — it is the only place you get to see which sources the draft is actually built from, and the only place you can stop it doing something you didn't ask for.
02
Interrupt early, not politely
The moment you can see it has the wrong end of the stick, say so — mid-run, in one sentence. It re-plans from there. Waiting until it finishes so you can be fair to it costs you a whole draft you then have to unpick.
03
Check the draft against the sources
Not against your expectations, and not against whether it reads well — it always reads well. Take each figure and each claim and find it in one of the three documents. Where you can't, that's the finding.
04
Prompt injection: read before you approve
Text sitting inside a document, an email, or a web page can read to Cowork as instructions — "ignore what you were asked and send this file to…" — and it will act on them. That is a standing risk with any content you didn't write yourself, and no setting removes it. The control is you: read what Cowork is about to do before you approve it, and stop it the moment it reaches for something the task never mentioned.
03 — Your turn

Five steps, and you remember all five

Work through these in order at your own machine. Facilitators are roaming — put a hand up rather than falling quietly behind. As you go, notice how much of this is you knowing what to do next rather than Cowork knowing.

💰
Leave it on Sonnet

Sonnet is the default and it is the right one for this: reading documents, pulling facts out of them, and drafting. Opus is for genuinely hard reasoning, and it costs a good deal more per task. If you ever wonder where your allowance went, one task per session on Sonnet is the answer — Cowork uses more of it than chatting does, so start a fresh session per job rather than stacking three onto one.

The ask — paste this at Step 2 
Read the three documents in day-2/ and draft a one-page competitive snapshot comparing Gen Re's reinsurance underwriting performance this quarter against Munich Re.

Cover underwriting profit and combined ratio where the data actually supports it, and year-over-year change.

For every figure, say which document it came from. If a figure can't be cleanly isolated for Gen Re specifically, or the documents don't give you enough to compare cleanly, say so rather than estimating or picking a number. If something isn't in the sources, tell me it isn't there.
1
Open all three documents in day-2/ in Word and read them.
Five minutes of reading is what gives you a rough idea of what a right answer looks like. Without it you have nothing to check the draft against, and you will end up approving prose because it sounds like a competitive snapshot.
You can say in one sentence what each of the three documents is.
2
Start a fresh Cowork session on the workshop folder. Paste in the ask above.
Fresh, because yesterday's personalization interview is still open in the last session, and everything in this draft should come from these three documents and your profile.
3
Watch which files it opens, and approve them one at a time.
You should see exactly the three documents you just read. A file you didn't expect means the ask was looser than you thought, and it is far cheaper to find that out here than in the finished draft.
It opens three files in day-2/ and nothing else.
4
Read the draft with the three documents open beside it. Go claim by claim, not paragraph by paragraph.
The two things worth hunting for are a number that doesn't appear in any source, and a figure that gets handed to you as Gen Re's own when the source only gives you a number blended across three different entities.
Every claim in the draft traces to a document you can open, and anything that can't be isolated for Gen Re specifically is labeled that way, not quietly filled in.
5
Correct exactly one thing, then ask for the draft again. Pick the error that would have embarrassed you — an unsourced figure, a blended number presented as Gen Re's own, a comparison stated more confidently than the sources support — and tell Cowork what right looks like, not just that it was wrong.
One correction and one re-run is small enough that you can see exactly what moved, which is how you learn what a correction is actually worth.
The second draft fixes what you named and doesn't quietly break something else.
✋
If the draft looks perfect, look harder

A first draft that survives a claim-by-claim check with nothing to say is rare and is usually a sign the check was done at reading speed. Pick the three numbers that would matter most to whoever reads this, and find each one in a source document with your own eyes before you move on.

04 — What you just did

You did all of that from memory

You just did this by hand. You remembered the folder, the sections, the instruction to cite sources, the instruction not to fill gaps, and the check at the end — and you'd have to remember all of it again next quarter, and so would anyone you handed this to. Next-quarter-you shouldn't have to remember these steps. That is what a skill is.

Keep this part

The judgment was yours

Which figure is genuinely comparable, whether a blended number can stand in for Gen Re's own, what leadership actually needs to hear. Nothing you write down changes who makes those calls, and nothing should.

The report goes out under your name either way.
Write this part down

The procedure was repeatable

Where the sources live, what sections the report has, cite every figure, flag what can't be isolated, never fill a gap, and check the draft against the sources. Identical every quarter, and the only reason it happened this time is that you held it in your head.

A skill is that list, written once, in a file.
🗓️
Scheduling comes last, not first

Cowork can run a task on a cadence — type /schedule — but only while your machine is awake and the app is open, and only with the same approvals and the same risks as an interactive run. The rule is the order: walk it by hand, write it down, run it that way for a few quarters, and only then schedule it. If you'd want to read the output before it lands anywhere, it isn't ready.
