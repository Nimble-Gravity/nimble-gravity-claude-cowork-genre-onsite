Day 3 · Lesson 1 of 2

Use-Case Ideation

This morning you don't brainstorm your use cases — you delegate the brainstorming. You'll install a skill called ng-brainstormer, sit a short interview about your actual week, and get back a ranked list of ten places to start, with the payoff arithmetic done and a recommended model for each, packaged as a deck. Then we take one item from one of those lists and start building it, live.

Use Cases + Governance · ~20 min
01 — The brainstormer

One skill that finds the other ten

ng-brainstormer is a Cowork skill built for exactly this session. It interviews you about your role, then hands back a slate of ten candidate skills — each with a value type, an ownership scope, a difficulty score, napkin-math ROI, and a recommended Claude model — and builds the deck while you get on with something else. Five steps, and we do them together in the room.

1
Download the skill. Grab ng-brainstormer.skill from the resource library — one file, and it's just a zip.
Inside is a complete, shipping skill: the SKILL.md you learned to read yesterday, plus the scripts that build the deck and the reference notes it reasons from. Worth browsing later for that reason alone — this is what a finished one looks like.
The file is in your Downloads.
2
Install it. Unzip it and drop the ng-brainstormer folder into your Cowork skills folder — the same place yesterday's skills live.
Yesterday you wrote a skill description and learned why the wording decides whether it ever fires. Today you're on the other side of one: this description triggers on the phrasings you'd actually type — "brainstorm skill ideas", "where should I start with Cowork".
Ask Cowork to "brainstorm Cowork skills for my role" and it announces the skill.
3
Sit the interview. Answer its questions about your actual week: the recurring meetings, what you're accountable for, who you answer to, which tools and data you touch.
It brainstorms from your week, not your job title — "underwriter" and "claims manager" cover wildly different Tuesdays. The notes you jotted as pre-work are the answers; and if your about-me/ folder from Day 1 is in reach, it reads that first and skips what it already knows.
It plays back a picture of your role you'd actually recognise.
4
Pick from the slate. It stops and shows you all ten in a table before going deeper. Choose the three to five that get the full treatment — workflow, data sources, ROI arithmetic, recommended model.
The checkpoint is in the skill on purpose: a deck built on the wrong ten ideas is wasted effort no matter how polished it looks. Nothing is thrown away — every idea stays in the deck's summary table; your picks are the ones that get a page of their own.
You've told it which ones to flesh out.
5
Let it build the deck. Kick off the build and leave it running — it writes the data file, runs the build script, and checks its own rendering, without you watching.
This is Day 1's lesson made physical: delegation means the work continues while you don't. The build runs through the guided build and the break, and your deck is done by the share-out.
A .pptx lands in your workshop folder.
🏛️
Shapes that have already worked here

The slate won't be guessing. Gen Re's own pilot community has already put Claude on this kind of work: IFRS 18 impact analysis in about an hour rather than half a day, an expected-loss view pulled out of a 70-tab submission in minutes, claims-outlier segmentation, and a disability-income claim form redesigned in roughly four minutes. Some of it came back clean and some needed real correction, which is rather the point — every one a strong first draft that a person read, corrected, and signed.

🔌
When a row says "needs a connector you don't have"

The skill flags any idea that depends on a data source that isn't connected in your session yet. That's it being honest, not it failing: the idea is real, it just isn't buildable today. Those rows are your "later" column — and whether that connector gets turned on is exactly the kind of question the governance snapshot at the end of today answers.

02 — Reading your slate

Ten ideas, ranked and priced

The ranking is 1 to 10 by how hard each would be to build and run reliably — not by how much it's worth. The spread is deliberate: some pure time-savers, some research and decision support, some output quality; some just for you, some your team could share, some the whole organisation could run. Two colour scales carry the judgement: gold for build complexity, green for the money. Four rows of the kind a reinsurance slate produces — invented cedent, invented numbers, real shapes:

Rank 2 · Time savings · Individual

Meeting notes to action items

Fires when — a cedent review or committee ends. Touches — your raw notes, the agenda, the previous action list. Produces — decisions and actions with named owners, in the format you already circulate. Model — a fast, cheap one: it runs often and the judgement is light. ROI — time-savings arithmetic: forty minutes, three or four times a week, at a loaded rate.

The classic rank-2: small, frequent, universally disliked — and the right place to start.
Rank 4 · Time savings · Individual → team

Quarterly experience summary

Fires when — quarter end; a natural /schedule candidate. Touches — the quarter's experience data, the pricing workbook output, last quarter's summary. Produces — this quarter's summary in the same sections and the same voice, movements called out. Model — mid-tier: synthesis in a house voice. ROI — most of a day, four times a year — and multiplied if the team adopts it.

Scheduled work is the easiest ROI arithmetic there is — the cadence is already on the calendar.
Rank 5 · Output quality · Team

A pack against a checklist

Fires when — a renewal or quarterly reporting pack arrives. Touches — the new pack, the version it replaces, the team's standard checklist. Produces — a table: each point present, missing, or changed, with the page reference and the figure quoted. Model — mid-tier: careful reading, little invention. ROI — an hour or two per pack, across everyone on the team who reads them.

Comparison work is where a tireless second reader earns its keep.
Rank 6 · Better decisions · Individual → team

Treaty-submission triage

Fires when — a submission pack arrives from a cedent. Touches — the pack, the appetite note for that class, last year's submission for the account. Produces — a two-page triage note: what's on offer, what's missing, what moved, the three questions to send back. Model — top-tier: this is the judgement-heavy one, closest to what you're actually accountable for. ROI — directional expected value, assumptions shown, not a promise.

The output is a note you send back, not a decision you delegated.
🧮
The ROI is napkin math, and it says so

Every estimate in the deck declares which of two things it is: a time-savings calculation (minutes saved × frequency × loaded rate, arithmetic shown) or a directional expected-value estimate (assumptions shown). The total slide sums only the ideas you picked for full treatment, so the headline can't drift from the pages behind it. Read it the way it's written — a ranking tool and a baseline, not an audited figure. The green scale is log-scaled for the same honest reason: a real slate spans a few thousand a year to a few hundred thousand, and on a linear scale everything but the outlier looks identical.

03 — From list to first build

Picking where to start

In a few minutes I'll take one item from one of the room's slates and show you how to go from a slate row to a working skill, live on the projector. These four tests are how that item gets picked — and they're the same four you'll apply this afternoon in the Advanced block, or at your desk on Monday, when you pick your own first build. A row needs all four.

01
Documents you could put in a folder right now
The slate names each idea's data sources — but "named" isn't "in hand". Not "I could probably export it": files that exist, that you can open, that you could copy into one folder before the break. Anything that first needs a system extract, a new report, or somebody else's permission is a fine second project and a poor first one.
02
An output you would recognise as right
Every slate row names its output format — a two-page note, a table, a summary in your standard sections. Hold it to that: if you can't say what lands at the end, you can't tell in the share-out whether it worked, and "it gave me some useful thoughts" is where sessions like this go to die.
03
Nothing confidential in it
The same rule as Days 1 and 2, and it doesn't relax because the work is real: follow Gen Re's own data-classification policy for what comes into this room. If your best row runs on documents that can't be here, keep the row — we build against a stand-in and you point it at the real folder back at your desk.
04
Start at your 2s and 3s, not your 8s
The difficulty ranking exists precisely for this moment. A low-ranked row does the same thing every time with sources that already exist — buildable this morning. Your 8s and 9s need new data sources or judgement calls halfway through; they're why the deck goes home with you, not why we're here till lunch. The first skill you finish teaches you how to build the ambitious one.
