Use-Case Ideation

You came in with a canvas — one piece of your own recurring work, written down in five lines. This morning the room reads them, picks one or two, and builds a working skill from them live. This lesson is how to read a canvas, what four filled-in ones look like, and what makes one buildable inside forty minutes.

Use Cases + Governance · ~20 min
01 — The canvas

Five lines about one piece of your work

The canvas is deliberately short: five fields, fifteen minutes, nothing to learn. It isn't a business case and nobody is scoring it. Its only job is to carry enough detail that somebody who doesn't do your job could tell whether Cowork's draft came back right. If yours is thin in places, fill the gaps now, before we read them together.

1
The workflow. Name it in one line, the way you'd say it to a colleague.
"The quarterly experience summary for a treaty" is a workflow. "Reporting" is a department. The narrower the line, the better the skill, because one that tries to cover four jobs does none of them well enough to trust.
Someone two desks away would know what you meant.
2
Its trigger. What starts it: an email lands, a date arrives, someone asks.
The trigger is what tells Cowork when this applies, and it is the sentence that decides whether the skill fires on its own next month instead of sitting unused because nobody remembered it was there.
You can finish the sentence "I do this whenever…" without hedging.
3
The documents it touches. Every source, and where each one lives.
This is the field people leave vaguest and the one that decides whether the skill works. Cowork can't find what you didn't name, so it improvises — and an improvised source is exactly the sort of error that reads perfectly well on the page.
Each source has a folder, a mailbox, or a system named next to it.
4
What “checked and correct” means. The test you apply before you sign it.
You already run this test, usually without writing it down: the totals tie, every figure traces to a source, nothing is asserted that the documents don't support. Written down, it becomes the thing the skill is built to satisfy and the thing you grade its output against.
It is a test someone else could run, not "it looks about right".
5
Time it takes today. A number, not a feeling: minutes or hours per run.
Two reasons. It tells the room which canvases are worth forty minutes of everyone's morning, and it is the only baseline you will have when somebody asks in three months whether any of this paid off.
You wrote down a number and how often the work recurs.
✏️
A thin canvas about real work beats a perfect one about none

Nobody is marking these. If you only half-filled yours, the two fields worth going back for are the documents and the check — those are the two the build actually depends on. The rest we can fill in together in the room, out loud, in about a minute each.

🏛️
Shapes that have already worked here

These aren't hypotheticals. Gen Re's own pilot community has already put Claude on this kind of work: IFRS 18 impact analysis in about an hour rather than half a day, an expected-loss view pulled out of a 70-tab submission in minutes, claims-outlier segmentation, and a disability-income claim form redesigned in roughly four minutes. Some of it came back clean and some of it needed real correction, which is rather the point — every one of them a strong first draft that a person read, corrected, and signed.

02 — Four canvases, filled in

What a good one looks like

Four worked examples, none of them carrying anything confidential — the cedent is invented and so are the numbers. Read them for shape rather than subject: recurring, document-heavy, and ending in something you could call right or wrong in a couple of minutes.

Canvas A

Treaty-submission triage

Trigger — a submission pack arrives from a cedent. Documents — the pack itself, the appetite note for that class, and last year's submission for the same account. Output — a two-page triage note: what's on offer, what's missing, what moved since last year, and the three questions to send back. Checked and correct — every figure traces to a page in the pack, and nothing missing is quietly filled in. Today — two to three hours per pack.

The output is a note you send back, not a decision you delegated.
Canvas B

Quarterly experience summary

Trigger — quarter end. Documents — the quarter's experience data, the pricing workbook output (the TreatyLayerPricer quarterly run), and last quarter's summary. Output — this quarter's summary in the same sections and the same voice, with the movements called out. Checked and correct — the totals tie back to the workbook, every movement has a number behind it, and anything the data doesn't explain is flagged rather than narrated over. Today — most of a day, four times a year.

The same shape every quarter is exactly what makes it worth writing down.
Canvas C

Meeting notes to action items

Trigger — the end of a cedent review or an internal committee. Documents — your raw notes, the agenda, and the previous meeting's action list. Output — decisions, actions with a named owner and a date, and anything left open, in the format you already circulate. Checked and correct — every action has an owner who was actually in the room, and no heading is padded with something nobody said. Today — forty minutes, usually the following morning.

Small, frequent, and universally disliked — the classic first skill.
Canvas D

A pack against a checklist

Trigger — a renewal or quarterly reporting pack comes in and has to be read through. Documents — the new pack, the version it replaces, and your team's standard checklist of what a complete pack contains. Output — a table: each checklist point marked present, missing, or changed, with the page reference and the figure quoted. Checked and correct — every row cites the page it looked at, and a “changed” row says what it changed from. Today — an hour or two, and it's the one people rush.

Comparison work is where a tireless second reader earns its keep.
03 — Picking the one we build

What makes a canvas buildable today

In a few minutes the room picks one or two canvases and we build a skill from them live, together, against a forty-minute clock. That clock rules out some genuinely good ideas, and being ruled out this morning says nothing about whether the work is worth handing over. Four tests, and a canvas needs all four.

01
Documents you could put in a folder right now
Not "I could probably export it" — files that exist, that you can open, that you could copy into one folder before the break. Anything that first needs a system extract, a new report, or somebody else's permission is a fine second project and a poor live build.
02
An output you would recognise as right
A named deliverable with a shape: a two-page note, a table, a summary in your standard sections. If we can't say what lands at the end, we can't tell in the share-out whether it worked — and "it gave me some useful thoughts" is where sessions like this go to die.
03
Nothing confidential in it
The same rule as Days 1 and 2, and it doesn't relax because the work is real: follow Gen Re's own data-classification policy for what comes into this room. If your workflow is a strong one but its documents can't be here, bring the shape and we'll build against a stand-in — you point it at the real folder back at your desk.
04
Doable in forty minutes
One workflow, one output, three or four sources. A skill that has to make a judgement call halfway through — which of these two treatments applies here — is a second skill, not a first one. Start with the version that does the same thing every time.
