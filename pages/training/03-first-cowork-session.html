Personalize & Verify

You'll watch the same document get summarized twice — once by a Cowork that knows nothing about you, and once after you've told it who you are and what you will not put your name to. In between, you run the interview yourself. The point of the hour is the difference between the two summaries, and what it takes to catch what's wrong in either.

Exercise · ~45 min
01 — Demo: the cold read

Read the document first, then ask

Watch this one — you don't need to type along. A facilitator opens the demo document in Word, scrolls it end to end, and says out loud what it is. Only then does Cowork get asked for anything. That order matters: you cannot check a summary of a document you have never looked at.

The document

demo/treaty-layer-summary.docx in your workshop folder — a quarterly treaty-layer summary for Meridian Mutual, a cedent we made up. Layer structure, attachment points, ceded premium, the quarter's loss activity, and a covering narrative. Ordinary work, ordinary length.

The ask

A one-page business summary someone could take into a quarterly review — the kind of thing you'd write on a Friday afternoon. Not a diagram, not a process map, not a restructuring of the document. Prose a colleague can read in two minutes.

The demo ask — run cold now, and again after you've personalized 
Read demo/treaty-layer-summary.docx and write me a one-page summary I can take into the quarterly review. Cover what the treaty covers, how the layers sit, how the quarter went, and anything I should raise before the meeting. Don't invent anything — if the document doesn't say, tell me it doesn't say.
🔎
Two things in this document don't agree

There is a total that doesn't sum and a date that contradicts one earlier in the file. They're in there on purpose. Cold, Cowork will most likely repeat both in fluent, confident prose — because that's what the document says, and nobody asked it to check. That is the whole lesson: it drafts, you verify, and the verification only happens if someone builds it into the ask.

02 — What to watch while it runs

Four moments worth your attention

A Cowork session isn't a black box that hands you a result. There are four points where you can see what's happening and change it, and knowing them is the difference between supervising the work and receiving it.

01
Open
Open the source document yourself first and scroll it. Thirty seconds of skimming tells you roughly what a right answer looks like — and without that, you have no basis to judge anything Cowork gives you.
02
Inspect
Cowork shows each file it's about to open or write, and waits. Read those prompts instead of clicking through them. If it reaches for a file you didn't expect, that's information — the ask was probably ambiguous.
03
Engage
You don't have to wait for it to finish being wrong. Say "stop — I meant the second layer, not the aggregate" mid-run and it re-plans from there. Correcting the plan costs far less than correcting the finished draft.
04
Check
Read the output next to the document, not next to your expectations. For each number and each claim, ask where it came from — and ask Cowork to tell you, if it didn't say.
03 — Choosing a permission mode

Begin with ask before acting

Permission mode is how much Cowork does without stopping to check with you. For today, and for any unfamiliar files, leave it pausing for approval on each action — that pause is where the inspecting happens.

Ask before acting

Approve each step

Cowork stops before each action and shows what it's about to do. Use it for anything new, anything you didn't write yourself, and anything you want to watch closely. It is the right default, and it's what we use all day today.

In either mode, it asks first before deleting a file for good.
Act without asking

Faster, when you're watching

Cowork runs the whole plan without pausing. Quicker for a job you've run before on files you trust, while you're sitting there watching it. Not for your first run, and not for anything that came from outside Gen Re.

Pick the mode by how much you trust the content.
04 — Your turn

Teach Cowork who you are

Now you run the interview. It asks about your role, who reviews your work, how you write, and what you'd never put your name to, then writes your answers into plain files in the workshop folder. You are not going to finish it today, and you don't need to — we run the first pass, you tell it to write the files with whatever it has when time is called, and everyone leaves with a working personalization. Deepening it is homework. Copy the prompt below, then work through the five steps, answering as yourself.

Paste into a fresh Cowork session on your workshop folder 
You're going to personalize Cowork for me. Run a thorough, adaptive interview, then write my personalization files to THIS folder so that from now on you know my role, my voice, my priorities, and my team. Treat it like onboarding a sharp new assistant — by the end you should be able to draft in my voice and tailor your help to my actual job.

STEP 1 — HARVEST FIRST
If any data source or working folder is already accessible, harvest everything you reasonably can about me BEFORE asking anything: my name, title, and role signals, the kinds of documents and data I work with, my current projects and priorities, and how I write (tone, length, sign-off) from any drafts you can read. Build a draft picture of me from this, and only ask me about what you couldn't find or need to confirm.
If nothing is connected, or you can't read anything useful, do NOT stop and do NOT error — just go to the interview and ask me directly to fill every gap. Either path must finish and produce the files.

STEP 2 — IDENTIFY MY ROLE AND DOMAIN
Work out what I actually do and in what field — underwriting, actuarial, claims, finance, legal, operations, or anything else. If the harvest told you, confirm it in a sentence; if not, ask. This shapes everything that follows.

STEP 3 — RUN AN ADAPTIVE INTERVIEW
Tailor the questions to my role and field — a treaty underwriter, a pricing actuary, a claims manager, a finance lead, and a compliance officer should each get a different conversation. Don't read from a fixed script: let each answer shape your next question, and generate the questions that actually matter for my work. (For pricing you'd dig into models, assumptions, and sign-off; for claims, case types, reserving practice, and cedent correspondence; for finance, close cycles, materiality, and who signs — use the same instinct for whatever I turn out to do.)

Run it well:
- Ask me ONE question at a time, using Cowork's question popup (the modal) — not by typing the questions inline in chat. Offer answer choices in the popup where they help, so I can just pick, and wait for each answer before asking the next.
- Confirm what you harvested; only ask for what you don't already know.
- Sharpen vague answers with follow-ups; get concrete examples, real names and numbers where they help.
- Respect my time. Ask your highest-value questions first, because I may stop you early and ask you to write the files with what you have — be ready to do that at any point. Left to run, the full interview is 20–40 minutes. If I skip something, put [FILL IN] there; never invent an answer.
- Read it back. Before writing any files, recap what you heard and let me correct it.

COVER THESE (adapt and prioritize to my role)
- Identity and role: name, title, team, who I report to.
- Accountability: in one sentence, the outcomes I own.
- Where my week goes: the 3–7 tasks or deliverables that take most of my time, and which recur.
- Priorities and current work: what I'm focused on now; active projects.
- Audience and stakeholders: who I produce work for; who I collaborate with; who reviews or approves it.
- Tools, data, and domain rules: the systems and data I live in, plus any standards or regulations that bind my work.
- Voice and writing style (spend real time here — ask several questions): how formal or casual I want drafts; sentence feel (short and punchy, or fuller and flowing); tone (warm, direct, neutral, authoritative); jargon vs plain language, and words I always use or always avoid; format defaults (bullets vs prose, length, headings, how I open and sign off); and how my voice shifts by audience (internal vs client, exec vs peer). Capture a short real sample of my own writing I'm happy with, confirmed by me, to anchor it.
- AI do's and don'ts (ask me directly): what you should ALWAYS do in my name (cite the source, flag uncertainty, ask before sending, keep it tight) and NEVER do (invent figures, make the decision, overclaim, be sycophantic). And the AI "tells" to avoid so drafts don't sound like AI — clichés and filler ("it's not just X, it's Y", "delve", "in today's landscape"), over-hedging, emoji, bullet overload, em-dash overuse.
- Standards: what "good" vs "rushed" looks like, and the review or approval steps I must respect.
- Durable facts: stable things worth remembering — fiscal year, recurring deadlines, naming conventions, regulatory constraints.

THEN WRITE THESE FILES TO THIS FOLDER (keep claude.md short and pointing to the about-me/ files; plain first person, concrete over generic):
- claude.md — short entry point: read the about-me/ files each session; who I am in a line; what I'm accountable for; default to my voice-profile and writing-rules for anything you draft; and keep about-me/memory.md current — whenever you learn a durable fact during a session, add or update it there, and review memory.md at the end of each session.
- about-me/about-me.md — role, team, reporting; accountability; where my week goes; current priorities; audience and stakeholders; tools, data, and domain rules; good vs rushed; my approval chain.
- about-me/voice-profile.md — my voice in detail: formality, sentence feel, tone, jargon level, the words I use and avoid, format defaults, and how it shifts by audience — plus a short real sample in my voice.
- about-me/writing-rules.md — explicit AI do's and don'ts: what to always do and never do in my name, my hard constraints, and the specific AI "tells" to avoid (clichés, filler, over-hedging, emoji, em-dash overuse).
- about-me/team.md — the people I work with and how (manager, collaborators, reviewers, audience); seed it from what you harvested; leave room to grow.
- about-me/memory.md — durable facts to remember across sessions; start with what I gave you. It is a living document: you add to it during and after each session as you learn key things (per the instruction in claude.md).

QUALITY BAR: specific over generic; use the real details you harvested or I gave you; mark gaps with [FILL IN] rather than padding. When done, list the files you wrote and tell me to start a fresh session and run two checks — "Recap what you know about me." and "Given who I am, what are the three highest-leverage things you could help me with this week?" If the playback is thin, point me to the file to fix.

START NOW: first tell me what you found about me from anything you can read (or say you couldn't read anything and will ask directly), confirm my role and field, then begin.

That's where the interview writes your files. Knowing the folder before you start is what lets you watch them appear rather than wonder where they went.

Start a fresh Cowork session on the workshop folder. Paste in the interview prompt above.
A fresh session means nothing from the demo is still in play. Everything the interview writes should come from your answers, not from a treaty summary it happened to read ten minutes ago.
3
Answer as yourself. Your real role, your real reviewers, the phrases you actually refuse to sign off on.
These files are yours and you keep them after the workshop. An invented answer produces a configuration that flatters someone who doesn't exist and helps you with nothing.
4
Work silently for five minutes. Facilitators are roaming — put a hand up rather than turning to your neighbour.
The interview is a conversation, and a conversation doesn't survive a room of thirty people talking over it. Five quiet minutes gets everyone through the questions that matter most, which is as far as we go today.
5
When time is called, tell Cowork to write the files with what it has. Read the recap, correct anything wrong, and let it save.
Cut the interview off mid-flight on purpose — a half-finished profile that exists beats a thorough one you never wrote. The gaps come back as [FILL IN] markers, and finishing them is tonight's homework. The read-back is the cheapest place to fix a misunderstanding, because once it's in voice-profile.md every draft you get for the rest of the week inherits it.
Ask "What do you know about how I work?" — the answer should quote your own about-me answers back at you, in your words. If it's generic, open the file and fix it.

"Skip" is a real answer

If a question needs a document you don't have to hand, say skip. It leaves a [FILL IN] marker you can search for later and moves on. A configuration with three gaps that you finished beats a perfect one you're still doing at the coffee break.

05 — Demo: the same ask, personalized

Same document, same question, different answer

Watch this one as well — hands off keyboards. The facilitator goes back to demo/treaty-layer-summary.docx with the same prompt as the cold run, on a machine that has now been personalized. Nothing about the document changed and nothing about the ask changed. The only difference is a set of files describing who the facilitator is and what they won't put their name to.

What changes

Bottom line first, in the facilitator's register, at the length they said they write. Their writing rules say never state a figure you can't source — so this run should re-add the totals and cross-check the dates, and at least one of the two planted problems should come back as a flagged question rather than a confident sentence. Should, not will: the profile is doing that work, and your own profile will only ask for what you told it to ask for.

What doesn't change

Your accountability. A personalized Cowork is a better first reader, not a signature. It will still miss things — including, some days, both of the ones planted here — and it is still your name on the summary that goes into the review. Personalization moves the work from "rewrite this" to "check this". That's the whole gain, and it's a big one.

🧾
Take the comparison seriously

With both summaries on screen, ask what you'd have caught reading only the first one. That answer — not the time saved — is what makes the case for this internally. Run the same comparison on your own profile as homework: same document, same prompt, and see what yours flags. Where it stays quiet, that's a line missing from your writing-rules.md.
