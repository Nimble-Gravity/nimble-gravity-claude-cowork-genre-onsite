# Day 2 — Skills & Everyday Workflows · Facilitator Script
### Claude Cowork · 2 hours, hands-on · **onsite + hybrid** (one room, facilitators roam)

> **Conforms to the Day 2 hub** (`pages/workshops/module-2-workshop.html#agenda`) minute for minute — the
> client's own six-segment agenda table, 2 hours. Detailed spine *under*
> [`facilitator-guide.html#workshop-2`](pages/workshops/facilitator-guide.html#workshop-2).

**Outcome:** every participant leaves with a working skill built from a real business workflow, plus a feel
for **when packaging something as a skill is worth it** — and, more importantly, for what they were carrying
in their head before they wrote it down.

**The one non-negotiable:** **manual first, then the skill.** Do not teach skill anatomy before the room has
felt the manual version. The twenty-five minutes of doing it by hand *is* the argument for writing it down.
If you teach anatomy first, the rest of the day is a features tour.

**Legend:** **[SAY]** talk track · **[DO]** you act · **[THEY]** participants act · **[NOTE]** facilitator note

## Manual · 0:10–0:35 (25 min) — Walk a real business workflow
*Follow along: [Lesson 1 §Your turn](pages/training/05-working-effectively.html#walk).*
**Protect this block. If you are behind, shorten the Teach block that follows — never this one.**

**[SAY] — mode:** *"Hands **on**, all twenty-five minutes. I'll set it up and then I'm walking around."*

### (a) Frame the job · 0:10–0:13
**[SAY]** *"Project Alpha is replacing a legacy treaty-layer pricing workbook with a supported platform. You
are the programme manager and the steering group meets on Thursday. You have three documents: a status note,
an email from the vendor, and an open-items list. You need a one-page status report by Friday. That is a real
shape of work and most of you do a version of it."*

**[SAY] — the habits, in one pass, before they start:** *"Four things while you work. Stay in **ask before
acting** — the pause is where you get to look. **Interrupt early, not politely** — say 'stop, that's the wrong
layer' mid-run; correcting the plan is far cheaper than correcting the draft. **Check the draft against the
sources**, claim by claim, not paragraph by paragraph. And **read anything before you approve it** — a
document can contain text that reads like an instruction, and you are the one clicking approve."*

**[SAY] — cost, one line:** *"Leave it on Sonnet. Fresh session per job."*

### (b) Read the sources — five minutes, silent · 0:13–0:18
**[THEY]** *"Open all three documents in `day-2/` **in Word** and read them. Five minutes, no talking. You
should be able to say in one sentence what each of the three is."*

**[NOTE]** This mirrors the Day 1 rule — you cannot check a draft against a document you haven't read. Hold
the silence. Circulate; this is also your window to fix the broken personalizations from the check-in.

### (c) The ask · 0:18–0:22
**[DO]** On the projector, paste the ask from
[Lesson 1](pages/training/05-working-effectively.html#walk) into a fresh session — show the Copy button, show
it twice — and read it out loud so the room hears what a well-shaped ask sounds like:

> *Read the three documents in `day-2/` and draft a one-page status report for Project Alpha. Cover: where the
> project stands, what moved this week, what is blocked and who owns it, and anything that needs a decision
> from the steering group. For every figure and every claim, say which of the three documents it came from. If
> the documents disagree with each other, say so rather than picking one. If something I asked for isn't in
> them, tell me it isn't there — don't fill the gap.*

**[SAY] — point at the last three sentences:** *"Look at what the last three sentences are doing. Source every
claim. Say so if the documents disagree. Tell me if it isn't there. **That is the checking, written into the
ask.** Yesterday the cold demo failed because nobody put those sentences in. Notice that you had to remember
to write them — hold on to that, it comes back in twenty minutes."*

**[THEY]** Run it. Watch which files it opens and approve them one at a time — you should see exactly three
files in `day-2/` and nothing else.

### (d) Check the draft — claim by claim · 0:22–0:32
**[SAY]** *"Now the actual work. Three documents open beside the draft. Go **claim by claim**, not paragraph
by paragraph. Two things are worth hunting for: a number that doesn't appear in any source, and **a point
where two of the documents disagree and the draft quietly picked a side.**"*

**[NOTE]** Do not say more than that. Let them find it.

**[SAY]** at ~0:28 if the room has gone quiet: *"If your draft looks perfect, look harder. Pick the three
numbers that matter most to whoever reads this and find each one in a source with your own eyes."*

<a id="the-trap"></a>
**[NOTE] 🪤 — the planted disagreement. Know it, don't leak it.**
The **vendor email** says the UAT environment lands **Friday 21 August**. The **status note** and the
**open-items list** both say **Monday 24 August**. That is planted, not a typo — two sources against one, so
most drafts will quietly settle on 24 August and say nothing about it, even though the ask explicitly said to
flag disagreement. **Nobody should "fix" it.** Do not correct it in the source files and do not give it away
before people have read their own draft. When someone finds it, get them to say it to the room. When the room
hasn't found it by 0:30, ask: *"Everybody — what date does your draft give for the UAT handover? Show of
hands, 21st. Show of hands, 24th."* The split in the room is the lesson.

### (e) One correction, one re-run · 0:32–0:35
**[THEY]** *"Correct exactly one thing and ask for the draft again. Pick the error that would have embarrassed
you — a wrong owner, an unsourced figure, a blocked item written as if it were on track — and tell it what
**right** looks like, not just that it was wrong."*

**[SAY] — the bridge, and land it slowly:** *"Stop there. Look at what you just did. You knew to read the
sources first. You knew to ask it to cite. You knew to check claim by claim. You knew a disagreement was worth
flagging. **You did all of that from memory, and you'll have to do it from memory again next Friday, and the
Friday after that.** The judgment was yours and it stays yours. The **procedure** — that's the part that's
repeatable, and that's the part worth writing down."*

## Teach · 0:35–0:55 (20 min) — What a skill is
*Follow along: [Lesson 2](pages/training/09-anatomy-of-a-skill.html).*

**[SAY] — mode:** *"Hands off for twenty minutes, then back on."*

**[SAY] — lead with what, then why:** *"A skill is those five steps, written into a file, that Cowork reads
and follows on its own. You don't invoke it by name. You ask for a status report the way you'd ask a
colleague, and it fires."*

### (a) Name the steps · 0:35–0:42
**[DO]** On the projector, write out the steps the room just did, in their words — ask them to call them out
and type them up:
1. Read the three sources.
2. Draft against a fixed shape: where we stand / what moved / what's blocked and who owns it / what needs a
   decision.
3. Source every figure.
4. Flag disagreements between sources instead of picking one.
5. Say what isn't there rather than filling the gap.

**[SAY]** *"That's half a page. That half page is the skill. Everything else is formatting."*

**[SAY] — where the inputs live, and what good looks like:** *"Two more things go in the file: where the
inputs live — the `day-2` folder, an inbox, a shared drive — and what a good output looks like versus a bad
one. That second one is the bit everybody skips and it's the bit that makes it usable."*

### (b) The description does most of the work · 0:42–0:50
**[SAY]** *"There is one part of the file that decides whether it ever fires, and it's the **description** at
the top. It has to contain the words you'd actually type. If your description says 'A skill for reporting,'
it will never fire, because nothing you ever type looks like that. If it says 'Use when someone asks for a
weekly status report, a project update, or a steering-group note' — it fires."*

**[DO]** Show the finished example in
[Lesson 2 §A worked example](pages/training/09-anatomy-of-a-skill.html#example). Read the description out
loud. **[DO]** Show it **again**, this time pointing at the specific trigger phrases inside it.

**[SAY] — repeat it back differently:** *"Say that the other way round: the description is not a summary for
a human. It's the sentence that has to match what you type. Write it in the words you'd use on a Thursday
afternoon."*

**[SAY] — the body, briefly:** *"Keep the body short. Under five hundred lines, and most good ones are far
shorter — Anthropic's own guidance is 'concise is key; the context window is a public good.' And explain the
**why** next to each rule instead of shouting in capitals, because a rule with a reason survives contact with
a situation you didn't anticipate."*

**[SAY] — evals before docs, one pass:** *"One counterintuitive rule, straight from Anthropic: **write the
tests before you write the documentation.** Decide how you'll know it worked — three real examples, and what
right looks like for each — before you polish the file. Otherwise you polish a thing that doesn't fire."*

### (c) How two stack together · 0:50–0:55
**[SAY]** *"Last piece before the lab. Skills chain. If you have one that evaluates something and one that
formats a client update, you can ask for both in a single sentence and Cowork runs the first, hands the output
to the second, and gives you one document. You don't orchestrate it. You ask for the outcome."*

**[NOTE]** That is the whole setup for Room 4 of the vault. Don't over-explain it here — the lab teaches it.

**[NOTE]** If someone asks *"is that an agent?"* — one sentence: *"Different word for something we're not
using today; a skill is a written-down procedure and that's all you need."* Move.

## Lab · 0:55–1:35 (40 min) — The Skill Vault
*[`escape-room/index.html`](escape-room/index.html) · facilitator view:
[`escape-room/admin.html`](escape-room/admin.html) · full logistics in
[`escape-room/README.md`](escape-room/README.md).*

**[SAY] — mode, and the frame:** *"Hands on for forty minutes and I want you in teams. This is a game with a
clock and a leaderboard, and the reason it's a game is that a broken skill teaches you more than a working
one, and nobody volunteers to debug a working one."*

### Setting it up · 0:55–1:00 (five minutes, and do it properly)
**[DO]** Teams of **two or three**. Mixed — put someone whose Day 1 went well with someone whose didn't.
**[DO]** Everyone opens `escape-room/` and enters a **team name**. The clock starts when they do, so hold the
room until every team has a name in the box. **[DO]** Confirm the file pack is downloaded — six files, from
the chat, the USB stick, or the hub.

**[SAY] — the scenario, thirty seconds:** *"A consultant ran the modernization delivery, rolled off, and
locked the team's playbook in a vault. Their skills are the only keys. Four rooms, forty minutes. **Each
room's code is produced by the work** — you can't guess it, you have to make the skill actually work. Two
hints per room; the second one costs you two minutes."*

**[SAY] — the rooms, one line each, no more:**
- *"Room 1 — run a skill somebody else wrote."*
- *"Room 2 — a skill that never fires. The description is sabotaged. Fix it."*
- *"Room 3 — write one from scratch."*
- *"Room 4 — chain two of them through a single ask."*

**[NOTE]** Do not walk through the rooms in advance. The room-2 sabotage (`description: "A skill."`) is the
same point you just taught, discovered rather than told, and telling them costs you the discovery.

### Running it · 1:00–1:35
**[NOTE] — roam.** Both facilitators on their feet the whole time. Do not stand at the front. Do not narrate.
Watch the [admin view](escape-room/admin.html) between tables: current room, elapsed time, hints used,
attempts. A team with high attempts and no hints is stuck and too proud to take one — go to that table.

**[NOTE] — the OneDrive gotcha, say it out loud before it bites.** If any team's files are in a synced folder,
Cowork may not see a file they just saved — it looks exactly like the skill failing. Say this to the room at
the start of the lab and again to any table that hits an inexplicable "file not found." Move them to a local
folder rather than debugging.

**[NOTE] — common snags:**
- *Room 1, skill won't install:* the folder name must match, `SKILL.md` goes **inside** a folder, and that
  folder gets added to Cowork.
- *Room 2, still doesn't fire:* their new description describes the skill instead of the request. Ask them:
  *"what would you actually type?"* — then have them put those words in.
- *Room 3, wrong format:* their skill didn't enforce the four sections. That's the lesson; point at their own
  instructions rather than fixing it for them.
- *Room 4:* chaining fails when one of the two earlier skills was never actually saved. Check both are loaded
  before debugging the chain.
- *A team is genuinely stuck past ~8 minutes in one room:* tell them to take hint two and eat the penalty.
  Finishing three rooms well beats stalling in room two.

**[NOTE] — remote attendees in the lab.** They cannot join a table. Pair each remote attendee with an in-room
team over chat, or run them as their own team with the moderator relaying. Decide this **before** the session,
not at 0:55.

**[NOTE] — hybrid airtime.** Ask the moderator to post into the chat at 1:15: *"remote — how's it going, what
room are you on?"* It is the only way you'll know.

**[SAY]** at 1:30: *"Five minutes. If you're mid-room, get to a stopping point — you don't need to finish all
four."*

## Debrief · 1:35–1:50 (15 min) — Good skill vs. so-so skill

**[SAY] — mode:** *"Hands off. Screens down, actually — I want you listening."*

### The shares · 1:35–1:44
**[NOTE] — cap it at FIVE shares, and say the cap out loud.** Thirty people in teams of two or three is ten to
fifteen teams; ninety seconds each is fifteen to twenty minutes and it eats the comparison below, which is the
point of the day. **Five shares × 90 seconds ≈ 8 minutes (1:35–1:44)**, leaving 1:44–1:50 for the comparison.

**[DO] — pick the five while you are roaming, not by asking for volunteers.** Selection rule, in this order:
1. One team that **finished all four rooms** — the target.
2. One team whose **Room 2 description fix** finally made it fire — the day's core lesson, discovered.
3. One team that **got stuck and knows why** — more useful than a success, and say so.
4. One **remote** team or attendee, read in by the moderator — take them **second or third in the order**,
   never last, because late shares get cut.
5. One **wildcard** — whatever you saw at a table that the room should hear.

**[SAY] — the cap, so nobody feels skipped:** *"Five teams are going to share, I picked them while I was
walking around, and it is not a ranking. **Everybody else: paste your skill's description line into the
chat** — that's the one line that decides whether a skill fires, and I want to see fifteen of them."*

**[DO]** Each share in this format — say the format before the first team goes, and hold them to it:
> **What the skill does · what you had to change to make it fire · one thing you'd fix if you had another ten
> minutes.**

### The comparison that matters · 1:44–1:50
**[SAY] — set it up:** *"Now the point of the whole day. Everybody built something that technically worked.
Some of those will still be working in a month and some won't. Here's the difference."*

**[DO]** Put two examples on the projector — one strong team share and one weaker one. Take them from what
you actually saw, not from a prepared pair. If you didn't spot a good contrast during the lab, use Room 2's
before/after description.

**[SAY] — the four tells, and lead with what to do:**
1. *"**It fires without being named.** If you have to say 'use the status-report skill', the description is
   wrong. Good ones fire off the words you'd normally type."*
2. *"**It says what good output looks like**, not just what steps to take. That's the line most people leave
   out and it's the one that makes the output usable."*
3. *"**It's short.** A long skill is usually somebody writing down every exception they could imagine instead
   of the procedure."*
4. *"**Somebody who isn't you can run it.** That's the real test. If it only works because you know what it
   meant, you've written a note to yourself, not a skill."*

**[SAY] — say it back:** *"So-so: it works when you drive it. Good: it fires on its own, it knows what right
looks like, it's short enough to read, and a colleague can run it. Four things."*

**[THEY]** *"Two minutes — run the knowledge check at the end of
[Skill Anatomy](pages/training/09-anatomy-of-a-skill.html) to lock it in."*

## Close · 1:50–2:00 (10 min) — Homework & preview

**[SAY] — homework, and be specific:** *"One applied task before Thursday. Take the workflow you brought
today — your real one, not Project Alpha — and write the half page: the steps, where the inputs live, and what
good output looks like. Don't write the skill file. Write the half page. That's the hard part and it's the
part we build from on Thursday."*

**[SAY] — the Day 3 pre-work, and flag it as the one that matters:** *"Second thing, and this one genuinely
affects Thursday: **pre-fill the use-case canvas.** Five lines — the workflow, what triggers it, the documents
it touches, what 'checked and correct' means, and how long it takes you today. Link is on the
[Day 3 hub](pages/workshops/module-3-workshop.html) and the
[pre-work page](pages/workshops/pre-work.html#day-3). We read them before Thursday and the room builds one of
them live, so a canvas that isn't submitted can't be the one we build."*

**[SAY] — office hours:** *"Office hours are `[FILL IN]`. Bring the thing that didn't work."*

**[SAY] — preview:** *"Thursday: you bring the use case, the room picks one, and we build it live. Then a
short governance segment — what's connected, who can turn what on, and where the data goes — so you can answer
the questions your team will ask you the moment you tell them what you did this week."*

**[DO]** Feedback link in the chat, and say it out loud. Ask the moderator to post it for remote.

**[NOTE]** Before you leave the room: note down which participants' personalization is still broken, and which
teams didn't get past Room 2. Both groups need a hand at the Day 3 open.

### Facilitator appendix

- **If you are behind, cut from the Teach block (0:35–0:55).** In order: the chaining sub-block (0:50–0:55 —
  the vault teaches it anyway), then the body/evals paragraphs. **Never** shorten the manual walk, and never
  start the vault late — the vault has its own 40-minute clock and it does not compress.
- **If the vault runs badly** (backend down, files missing), fall back: teams work Rooms 2 and 3 as plain
  exercises from the [file pack](pages/workshops/module-2-workshop.html#materials) with no codes, and you
  debrief on the same four tells. The learning survives without the game.
- **A team finishes early.** Send them to Room 4 variations: *"now make it work on a document it's never
  seen."* Don't let them coach other tables — that removes the discovery.
- **Asked about scheduling / automating it.** One sentence: *"You can, and you shouldn't until the manual
  version is reliable — automating something you haven't checked just produces wrong answers faster."* Parking
  list.
- **Asked "skill vs. agent vs. connector."** One sentence, then move. It is on the parking list, not in this
  session.
- **Lines worth keeping verbatim:** *"you did all of that from memory"* · *"the judgment is yours, the
  procedure is repeatable"* · *"the description is not a summary for a human, it's the sentence that has to
  match what you type"* · *"if it only works because you know what it meant, you've written a note to
  yourself."*
